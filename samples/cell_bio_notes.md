# BIOL 204 — Cell Biology Notes

## Lecture 4: The plasma membrane

- Fluid mosaic model: the membrane is a fluid phospholipid bilayer with proteins and cholesterol floating in it. Lipids and many proteins can move laterally within the bilayer.
- The phospholipid bilayer is **selectively permeable**. Hydrophobic tails face inward, hydrophilic heads face the aqueous cytosol and extracellular fluid.
- Small nonpolar molecules such as O2 and CO2 cross freely. Ions and large polar molecules cannot cross unaided.
- Cholesterol sits between phospholipids and stiffens the membrane at low temperature, keeping it fluid at high temperature.
- Integral (transmembrane) proteins span the bilayer permanently. Peripheral proteins attach to the surface or to integral proteins.

## Transport proteins

- Channel proteins form a hydrophilic pore. Movement is passive, down a concentration gradient. Channels can be gated by voltage, ligand binding, or mechanical stretch.
- Carrier proteins bind their solute and change conformation to move it. Slower than channels but highly selective.
- Pumps are carrier proteins that move solute against its gradient, using ATP. The sodium-potassium pump is the standard example.

## Lecture 5: Membrane transport processes

- Simple diffusion: molecules move down their own concentration gradient, no protein involved. Rate depends on the size of the molecule and the steepness of the gradient.
- Facilitated diffusion: a polar or charged molecule moves down its gradient, but only through a channel or carrier protein. Still passive, still no ATP.
- Osmosis is the diffusion of water across a membrane. A hypotonic solution has lower solute concentration, so water moves into the cell and it swells. A hypertonic solution has higher solute concentration, so water leaves the cell and it shrivels.
- Active transport moves a solute against its concentration gradient and therefore costs energy.

## Primary and secondary active transport

- Primary active transport hydrolyses ATP directly to move the solute. The sodium-potassium pump spends one ATP to export 3 sodium ions and import 2 potassium ions. This maintains a low intracellular sodium concentration and is why nerve cells can fire.
- Secondary active transport uses the energy stored in an ion gradient rather than spending ATP directly. The solute hitchhikes along with the ion that is already moving down its gradient. The sodium-glucose cotransporter in the intestine is the standard example.
- Secondary active transport is still active because the ion gradient itself was created by spending ATP.

## Bulk transport

- Phagocytosis: the cell engulfs a large solid particle into a vesicle. Used by macrophages and white blood cells.
- Pinocytosis: non-specific uptake of extracellular fluid and dissolved solutes into small vesicles.
- Receptor-mediated endocytosis: specific uptake. Receptors bind a ligand, cluster in a coated pit, and the pit pinches off as a coated vesicle. Clathrin is the main coat protein.
- Exocytosis: vesicles fuse with the plasma membrane and release their contents outside the cell. Used for secretion of hormones and neurotransmitters, and for adding new membrane during cell growth.

## Lecture 6: The mitochondrion

- Double membrane. The outer membrane is permeable to small molecules. The inner membrane is impermeable to almost everything and folds inward into **cristae**, which massively increase surface area.
- The space between the two membranes is the intermembrane space. The interior is the matrix.
- The mitochondrion has its own circular DNA and 70S ribosomes, and it divides independently of the cell. This supports the endosymbiotic origin of the cell.
- The inner membrane carries the electron transport chain complexes I to IV, ATP synthase, and the transport proteins that move protons.

## Chemiosmosis

- The electron transport chain uses energy from electrons (from NADH and FADH2) to pump protons from the matrix into the intermembrane space.
- This builds an electrochemical proton gradient across the inner membrane: high H+ concentration outside, and the intermembrane space is electrically positive relative to the matrix.
- Protons can only flow back into the matrix through ATP synthase, because the inner membrane is otherwise impermeable to them.
- The energy of that flow drives ADP + Pi into ATP. This is chemiosmosis, and chemiosmotic coupling is the reason oxidative phosphorylation is so efficient.

## The electron transport chain

- Complex I: NADH dehydrogenase. Accepts electrons from NADH and pumps protons.
- Complex II: succinate dehydrogenase. Accepts electrons from FADH2. **It does not pump protons.** This is why FADH2 yields less ATP than NADH.
- Complex III: cytochrome bc1. Pumps protons and passes electrons to cytochrome c, a mobile carrier in the intermembrane space.
- Complex IV: cytochrome c oxidase. Passes electrons to oxygen, reducing it to water. Pumping at this complex consumes the oxygen.
- The chain regenerates NAD+ and FAD, which is why respiration is cyclic and depends on those carriers being recycled.

## Lecture 7: Aerobic respiration

- Glycolysis happens in the **cytosol**, not the mitochondrion. One glucose becomes two pyruvate, netting 2 ATP and 2 NADH. It does not require oxygen.
- Pyruvate oxidation: each pyruvate enters the matrix and becomes acetyl-CoA, releasing CO2 and producing 1 NADH each. This is where carbon dioxide first appears.
- The citric acid cycle (Krebs cycle) is in the matrix. Per glucose it yields 2 ATP, 6 NADH, and 2 FADH2, and releases 4 CO2.
- Oxidative phosphorylation (electron transport chain plus chemiosmosis) runs at the inner mitochondrial membrane and is the only stage that consumes oxygen directly.
- Overall yield per glucose is roughly 30 to 32 ATP, so the two stages before the chain are a small fraction of the total.
- Anaerobic glycolysis in yeast and in our own muscle fibres when oxygen runs out regenerates NAD+ by converting pyruvate to lactate, but yields only 2 ATP.

## Key terms to memorise

- Amphipathic: having both a hydrophilic head and hydrophobic tails. All phospholipids are amphipathic, which is why they form bilayers.
- Hypertonic / hypotonic: describes solute concentration relative to the cell, and therefore the direction of water movement.
- Translocase: a carrier protein that moves a specific solute across a membrane.
- Cristae: the folds of the inner mitochondrial membrane.
- Chemiosmosis: using a proton gradient to drive ATP synthesis.
